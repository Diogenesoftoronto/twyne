**Astra’s recommendation: turn Sentence Bench into a place to try your own revision and inspect what it changes.** Its central question should be: **“Did I improve this sentence, or accidentally change what I meant?”**

That is a narrower, testable purpose than helping with any difficult sentence. It also makes better use of the strongest existing work: anchored originals, editable trials, contextual previews, differences, and Undo.

This is an independent product hypothesis. I reviewed the requested files, callers, product documentation, Impeccable guidance, tests, and a checked-in visual baseline. I made no edits and did not validate the current interface in a live browser.

The current feature does more than its name makes apparent:

- **Entry:** selecting text exposes Rework or Alternatives. Automatic offers can follow repeated settled rewrites or moves, subject to settings and flow suppression. Manual opening can fall back to a sentence chosen by the controller. [Selection actions](/home/diogenes/Projects/twyne/src/components/editor/selection-actions.tsx:210), [controller](/home/diogenes/Projects/twyne/src/components/editor/extensions/struggle-tracker.ts:1414).
- **Rewrite:** an editable working copy, earlier settled wording, and local transformations such as removing “very” or replacing “in order to.” A configured prose model supplies additional alternatives. Its prompt assumes the writer keeps rewording and requests different approaches without explanations. [Local candidates](/home/diogenes/Projects/twyne/src/utils/sentence-bench.ts:84), [generation prompt](/home/diogenes/Projects/twyne/src/utils/in-flow-tools.ts:320).
- **Words, Place, Hear:** a small bundled thesaurus with optional local predictions; possible positions within the paragraph, without semantic ranking; and narration of the working sentence with its neighbours. These are distinct capabilities assembled into one surface. [Component](/home/diogenes/Projects/twyne/src/components/in-flow/sentence-bench.tsx:439), [placement rules](/home/diogenes/Projects/twyne/src/utils/sentence-bench.ts:457).
- **Decision and application:** word differences, optional comparison, ghost previews, an editor invitation, stale-source checks, and an undoable replacement. Applying even the writer’s own wording requires terminal punctuation and a successful grammar check. [Application](/home/diogenes/Projects/twyne/src/components/editor/extensions/struggle-tracker.ts:1074).

A writer could use it to recover an earlier wording, recall a word, hear cadence, or inspect an unfamiliar edit. Those are credible uses. The problem is that opening the tool does not establish which decision matters.

My diagnosis is that **the feature supplies alternatives before establishing a reason to prefer one**. Routine changes are easier to type directly. Difficult changes often concern emphasis, implication, argument, or narrative intention, which the tabs do not resolve. Context starts collapsed, although the quality of the decision depends on it. Independent rule candidates also start from the original, so choosing another can replace the previous trial rather than combine improvements. [Working-copy state and context](/home/diogenes/Projects/twyne/src/components/in-flow/sentence-bench.tsx:75).

The most consequential gap is specific: optional meaning judgements attach to supplied candidates, while a writer’s manually edited trial receives a grammar check at application. The more author-controlled path gets less help understanding consequences. Even candidate judgements produce a probability, rather than identifying what qualification or reference changed. [Meaning judgement](/home/diogenes/Projects/twyne/src/components/editor/extensions/struggle-tracker.ts:959).

The repository already distinguishes working controls from demonstrated writing quality. Its evaluation corpus is authored and fictional; passing those checks establishes useful mechanics, not demand. [Evaluation boundaries](/home/diogenes/Projects/twyne/docs/instrument-evaluation.md:15).

The recommended interaction is:

1. **Trigger:** the writer selects a sentence and chooses **Try a revision**. This is deliberate revision work; remove automatic opening based on rewrite count.
2. **Input and context:** preserve the exact original and its rich-text range. Show its paragraph immediately. The writer edits one trial, initially copied from the original. No prompt-writing exercise or automatic generation is required.
3. **Visible output:** show the trial in the paragraph, with an Original/Trial toggle and optional word differences. An explicit **Check what changed** action examines that exact pair and surrounding context.
4. **Useful model response:** at most two material observations, each tied to exact original and trial phrases. For example: “Removing ‘may’ makes this a definite claim.” Separate the observable edit from the model’s interpretation. Do not provide a quality score, a “safe” badge, or another batch of rewrites.
5. **Writer decision:** edit the trial, knowingly retain its changed meaning, or keep the original. Meaning changes can be intentional. Grammar and model observations advise; source-integrity checks enforce the application boundary.
6. **Return:** **Use revision** applies one undoable transaction, closes the surface, and restores the cursor to the revised passage. **Keep original** closes without changing the manuscript. A changed source invalidates application while preserving the trial for recovery.

The benefit is a concrete account of a revision’s consequences. Merely moving the current candidate cards into a cleaner layout would not deliver it.

Consider this **fictional journey**, offered as a design example rather than observed research:

A writer is tightening a local-government article:

> The council tested free evening buses on two routes last winter. **Ridership rose on the pilot routes, but the evaluation could not separate the effect of free fares from the longer service hours.** The council will decide next month whether to expand the programme.

They try:

> Free fares increased ridership.

In today’s bench, they can inspect the textual difference and apply the sentence after grammar checking. The important question remains unanswered.

In the proposed flow, **Check what changed** returns one observation:

> **“Could not separate the effect” → “Free fares increased”**  
> The trial attributes the increase to free fares. The original explicitly leaves their contribution unresolved against longer service hours.

The writer revises their own trial:

> Pilot-route ridership rose, but the evaluation could not separate the effects of free fares and longer hours.

They read the paragraph, apply the revision, and continue. The tool helped preserve a distinction during tightening; it did not make them choose among three stylistic performances.

There is relevant precedent, with clear limits. Scrivener’s official documentation describes comparing saved versions and restoring earlier text, supporting the familiarity of reversible experimentation. It does not establish demand for this sentence-level interaction. [Literature & Latte](https://www.literatureandlatte.com/blog/how-to-manage-compare-and-restore-snapshots-in-your-scrivener-projects). Sommers’s 1980 revision study distinguishes lexical substitutions from revisions concerned with argument and readership. That is historical qualitative evidence for broadening the revision question, not contemporary validation of Twyne. [Original paper](https://gossettphd.org/library/rhet-comp/sommers_revisionstrategies.pdf).

For a small first scope, retain the existing selection anchor, working copy, contextual preview, diff, stale checks, and Undo. Replace the four-tab body with one trial and one explicit comparison request. Start with one source sentence, allowing its replacement to contain multiple sentences. Validate model-returned evidence against code-owned spans. A successful evidence lookup establishes that the quotation exists, not that the interpretation is correct.

Remove from this surface:

- Automatically generated rewrite lists and elementary phrase substitutions.
- The thesaurus and unranked placement catalogue.
- Separate comparison cards below the main working copy.
- Numerical meaning confidence.

Existing read-aloud and editor actions can remain available through their established entry points. This proposal requires no redesign of persona feedback cards. Offline, the trial and literal comparison remain available; the semantic reading is explicitly unavailable.

Evaluation should answer whether this deserves to exist:

- **Decision value:** compare ordinary editing plus Undo, the local trial alone, and the trial with semantic observations. Use writers’ actual troublesome revisions. Count prevented *unwanted* meaning changes and useful decisions to keep the original.
- **Observation quality:** evaluate negation, uncertainty, quantities, actors, timing, intentional changes, and qualifications preserved elsewhere in context. Measure false alarms as well as missed changes. Require exact evidence references; never treat matched references as semantic verification.
- **Interaction cost:** measure time to a satisfactory decision and return to writing. Faster application alone is insufficient. Check keyboard use, narrow screens, marks, citations, stale edits, and one-step Undo.
- **Return value:** ask whether writers voluntarily reopen it for another real revision and whether the chosen wording survives later review. The existing docs also recommend retained edits and resumed writing over opens. [Proposed product measures](/home/diogenes/Projects/twyne/docs/writing-instruments.md:304).

The strongest objection is that **ordinary editing and Undo may already be enough**. If the semantic readout mostly states obvious differences, the separate bench should be retired rather than expanded.

Other unresolved tradeoffs remain. This direction helps evaluate an edit; it does not supply the missing idea when a writer is stuck. It favours precision-sensitive revision over exploratory composition. Some participants in the 2022 Wordcraft workshop did use generated prose extensively, so excluding generation sacrifices a real use case documented elsewhere; that LaMDA study does not establish what current frontier models can do. [Wordcraft study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.05030).

Finally, a model can invent a meaningful distinction or overlook one recovered elsewhere in the manuscript. The design must make disagreement inexpensive and avoid promising verification. The first research question is whether this focused comparison improves the writer’s decision enough to justify leaving ordinary editing.

## Why a writer does not reach for it

**It detects and proposes but never diagnoses.** Flower, Hayes, Carey, Schriver and Stratman separate detecting that something is wrong, diagnosing what is wrong, and choosing a strategy, in "Detection, Diagnosis, and the Strategies of Revision", College Composition and Communication 37(1), 1986 ([doi:10.2307/357381](https://doi.org/10.2307/357381)). I verified the citation but could not load the full text in this session, so I am leaning on the paper's widely cited distinction rather than quoting it. The bench does detection well ("You've reworded one sentence 4 times") and jumps straight to candidates. A writer on their fourth version has already rejected three wordings. Showing those back, plus surface transforms and three unlabelled model sentences, gives them nothing they did not have.

**It is built for the rewording model of revision.** Sommers found that student writers treat revision as rewording while experienced writers revise for meaning and the reader, in "Revision Strategies of Student Writers and Experienced Adult Writers", CCC 31(4), 1980 ([ERIC EJ240356](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ240356), [doi:10.2307/356588](https://doi.org/10.2307/356588)). Every pane of the bench operates at the word or wording level. The one thing it could say about meaning, the Jev probability, is hidden in a disclosure.

**It carries a lot of surface for one sentence.** Four tabs, a textarea that duplicates the editor, Compare, two context toggles, an About disclosure and a room. The manuscript already offers in-place editing with undo. The bench's genuine advantages over the editor are the ledger history, ghost preview, and Hear. The rest is weight.

**Every item is hedged and nothing is recommended.** The provenance honesty is right for this product, but a list where every entry says "meaning has not been checked" leaves the writer exactly as undecided as before. Inference, not evidence: I believe this is the root of "I cannot see myself using it". There is no user research behind that claim.

**It fires late and then nags.** The trigger needs three settled wordings, a five second settle each, and the Jev gate. By then the writer is either done or deep in it. Dismissal cools the paragraph for five minutes and the same card returns.

The ledger holds something no other writing tool has: successive settled wordings of one sentence. Diffing those wordings against each other, not against the current text, yields a diagnosis in pure code: the stable core the writer never touched, and the contested span they keep rewriting.

**Trigger.** Same settled-wording signal, but the margin tick's reason names the finding: "Four wordings. The opening clause has stayed; the ending keeps changing." Per the autonomy memory, that line appears with no click. The bench opens on click, or from "Rework" when the ledger has at least two wordings for the selected sentence.

**Input and context.** The ledger entry's wordings with timestamps, one neighbouring sentence on each side, and the dossier goal and audience. Jev when available. A prose provider when configured.

**Visible output, one block, no tabs.**

## 1. What the feature actually does

**How it opens**
- **From a selection.** Selecting text shows **Rework** (several words) or **Alternatives** (one word) in the selection toolbar (`selection-actions.tsx:210-217`). This calls `openKind("sentence-lab", text)`, which narrows to a single sentence: the one at the cursor, a text match, or the paragraph's longest (`struggle-tracker.ts:1412+`, `in-flow-tools.ts:203`). If you select a whole passage and click Rework, you quietly get a bench for one sentence of it.
- **Automatically, after rewording.** It opens when the struggle tracker sees the same sentence reworded 3 or more times. Jev decides whether to show it, and if Jev is unavailable the tracker shows it anyway because the local signal is strong enough (`struggle-signals.ts`, `audit.md:239`).
- **Automatically, after moving.** It opens on the Place tab when a sentence has been moved to 3 or more positions (`struggle-tracker.ts:318-336`).

**What's on the bench** (`sentence-bench.tsx`, `utils/sentence-bench.ts`)
- **Rewrite tab.** Up to 8 local candidates:
  - the writer's earlier "settled" wordings, taken from the sentence ledger;
  - 11 regex wordiness rules ("in order to" → "to", "made a decision to" → "decided to");
  - removing "really/very";
  - splitting at a semicolon;
  - moving a trailing "before/after/when" clause to the front.

  If a prose model is connected, it adds up to 3 alternatives from a generic prompt ("keep its meaning and register… reorder, cut, make concrete"). Every candidate is grammar-checked with Harper, shown as a diff with a word-count change, previewed as ghost text on hover, and carries an "About" disclosure full of caveats.
- **Words tab.** A hand-written thesaurus of roughly 45 headwords (walked → strolled/strode/wandered, good → sound/effective/valuable). There's also an optional on-device word-prediction model that shows a likelihood score.
- **Place tab.** Every neighbouring position in the same paragraph, generated by rule. The panel itself says "Their fit has not been judged."
- **Hear tab.** Text-to-speech of the sentence before, the working wording, and the sentence after.
- **Around the tabs.** Compare, Context, Whole paragraph, and Ask an editor (one persona comment).
- **Applying.** "Use this wording" applies the change with one undo and a stale-text check.

**Facts that limit it**
- The sentence ledger lives only in memory inside the struggle tracker; no other source file uses it. Version history is gone after a reload, which is usually when revision happens.
- The fixture sentence ("We made a decision to leave in order to find a very quiet room.") is written to trigger the rules. Sentences people actually agonise over almost never contain "in order to".

## 2. Why a writer would or wouldn't use it

**Why not (my inference from the code, not user research):**
1. **It answers the wrong question.** Rewording a sentence nine times rarely means the wording has a mechanical fault. Usually the writer hasn't decided what the sentence has to do: what it takes over from the sentence before and what it sets up for the one after. Wordiness rules and a thesaurus fix clutter, which the writer could fix alone.
2. **The writer has to diagnose the problem first.** Four tabs plus Compare, Context, Whole paragraph, Ask an editor and the About disclosures mean choosing between Rewrite, Words, Place and Hear before getting any help. That diagnosis is the hard part.
3. **Its most valuable data is just another list item.** No other tool knows "you've written this sentence six ways," yet the bench shows those versions as candidates beside regex output. It doesn't use them as evidence of what the writer is deciding between.
4. **With a model, it becomes the generic rewrite picker.** Three wordings in someone else's voice. Reza et al. find that form-focused writers, such as creative writers, want control over drafting and revision. Dhillon et al. found next-sentence suggestions didn't improve quality or productivity. That finding is adjacent rather than direct, because they tested continuation, not rewording.
5. **Caveats on every row.** "Meaning has not been checked" is repeated throughout. This is the reflexive hedging the project already wants to avoid.
6. **Place and Hear add little.** Place lists positions without judging them, which isn't much better than cut and paste. Hear duplicates "Hear it read".

**Why a writer would use it:** in-place ghost preview between the real neighbouring sentences, a one-undo apply, hearing the sentence in context, and the ledger of the writer's own versions. Those are worth keeping.

## 3. Recommendation: from "suggest alternatives" to "settle this sentence"

The job is to help the writer **decide between versions they've already written, or park the decision and keep drafting**. It isn't to generate more versions.

**Trigger**
- Same signal as today: a sentence with 3 or more settled wordings in the ledger.
- Change what appears: a quiet margin mark ("6 versions") instead of a four-tab panel opening mid-flow. The analysis runs on its own, which matches the autonomy-by-default memory. Opening it takes one click or one keystroke.
- Manually: Rework on a selection of **one** sentence. If the selection spans several sentences, offer the passage tools instead of quietly narrowing to one.

**Input and context**
- The ledger's wordings for the sentence, plus the current one.
- The sentence before and the sentence after.
- The brief's goal and audience. These are already passed to `classify` and `fillSpec`.

**What the writer sees**
1. The current sentence in place, with its neighbours shown faintly above and below.
2. One computed line (local, no model), built by aligning every version against the current one with the existing `sentenceWordDiff`. For example: *"Same in all 6: river · town · memory. Still changing: the verb (carries / holds / keeps) and the ending."* This names the decision the writer is circling.
3. The versions stacked oldest to newest, with the changing parts highlighted. Hovering shows the ghost preview in the page, which already exists.
4. If a model is connected, one sentence that frames the choice instead of a rewrite. For example: *"These differ on whether the river acts on the town or stores it; your next sentence ('Every spring it gives something back') assumes it acts."* No new prose is generated.

**What the writer decides**
- **Use** a version. This calls the existing `applyVariant`.
- **Hear A/B.** The current version, then the chosen one, each between the same neighbours. This extends `hearVariant` to play two in sequence.
- **Edit** in the working field. Each edit adds to the stack.
- **Park.** Keep the current wording and attach the runner-up versions to the sentence as a note. The margin mark changes from "versions" to "parked". This is the action I'd treat as most important: it lets one sentence stop holding up the draft.

**Back to the manuscript:** Use or Park closes the bench, returns focus to the editor with the cursor at the start of the next sentence, and stops the trigger from firing again for this sentence.

## 4. Before and after: one realistic session

**Before.** Morgan is drafting an essay opening and has reworded its second sentence six times. Rewrite opens on its own. No rule applies. Five earlier versions are listed as diffs beside "Read in context; meaning has not been checked." "Carries" isn't in the Words thesaurus. Place lists three positions. Morgan closes the panel and rewrites a seventh time. With a model connected, three polished rewrites appear in a register that isn't Morgan's, and Morgan picks none of them.

**After.** A "6 versions" mark appears beside the sentence. Morgan clicks it. The bench says *the verb keeps changing (carries / holds / keeps); everything else is settled*. Morgan hovers version 3 and sees it in the page, then hears the current version and version 3 back to back between the real neighbours. Morgan picks version 3 and clicks Use. The bench closes and the cursor sits at the next sentence. On a different day Morgan can't decide, clicks Park, and keeps drafting. The alternative waits on the sentence for the revision pass.

- **Keep:** the working field, ghost preview, one-undo apply, stale checks, Hear (now A/B), and Ask an editor, rephrased as "Which version does this job here?"

## 7. How to evaluate it

**Before building:** sample real ledger histories from the user's own sessions. If most struggling sentences don't have 3 or more clean versions (the ledger waits 5 s before counting a wording as settled, and history is in memory only), the feature has nothing to work with and the plan should change.

**Behaviour, measured locally:**
- After the bench opens on a struggle sentence: the share of opens that end in Use or Park versus closing without acting.
- Re-struggle rate: is the sentence reworded again within about 10 minutes of being settled? Lower is better.
- Time until the writer is typing elsewhere.
- The share of applied wordings that are the writer's own.

**The real test:** does the user reach for it on their own manuscript within a week without being prompted?

**Correctness:** fixture tests for the "same / still changing" summary using real version histories, not the rule-bait sentence.

## 8. Challenging this recommendation, and open tradeoffs
- **The bench may be the wrong layer altogether.** Experienced writers mostly revise at the level of ideas and the reader, not words (Sommers, 1980; details in Sources). The honest alternative recommendation is **no bench at all**: an inline "6 versions · hear · park" affordance on the sentence, with the effort moved to the paragraph tools (Claim Check, Reader Questions, Threads). My proposal is already half that. The cross-critique round should push on whether the panel needs to exist.
- **The "same / still changing" summary is untested.** It could be a clever display that tells the writer what they already know. The evidence for a stack of adjacent variations compared in context (ABScribe) involved LLM-generated variations, not the writer's own.
- **Removing model rewrites hurts some users.** Dhillon et al. found that higher scaffolding helped non-regular writers most. Dropping rewrites trades away a quick fix for those users in exchange for ownership.
- **Persistence.** The ledger is in memory only. Persisting versions (locally, or synced) is the bigger and more valuable change, but it raises a "where does this data go" question that PRODUCT.md says must be made explicit.
- **Auto-open versus a margin mark.** A mark is quieter, but autonomy-by-default argues for showing the analysis without a click.
- **Single-word Alternatives** needs a home or a decision to cut it.
- **Park as a comment** reuses existing infrastructure, but it mixes machine-made alternatives with human comments. A dedicated annotation type would be cleaner and larger.
- **The model's decision line is still a model judgement.** It may confidently misread the sentence. It intrudes on ownership less than generated prose does, but it isn't free.
